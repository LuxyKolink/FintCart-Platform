\appendix
\section{Anexos}
\label{anex:anexos}

An annex earns its place by shortening the distance between a sentence and the artifact that
supports it. Almost nothing supporting this report lives inside the report: the instrument set,
the formula language, the requirement statements, the protocol definitions and the bibliographic
ledger all live in the repository, under version control, beside the code that produced them.
Reprinting them here would create a second copy that can disagree with the first, and a copy
printed in a PDF cannot be re-run. What follows is an index in three parts: where each annexed
artifact lives and what a reader needs it for, the provenance of the bibliography, unusual enough
to be an annex in its own right, and the commands that rebuild every number the report quotes.
The third column of Table~\ref{tab:annex-index} exists so that this is not a table of contents
with extra steps.

\subsection{What is annexed, and where it lives}

Three rows of Table~\ref{tab:annex-index} need a sentence of justification. The validation
instruments are pointed at rather than summarised because a summary of an instrument is a
different instrument: the exact wording of a Likert item and the exact order of a task are the
measurement, so paraphrasing them destroys the only thing a reviewer can audit. The protocol
definitions are the authority over the prose for the reverse reason: where a chapter and a
\code{.proto} file disagree about a field name, the file is right, and the reader needs to know
which document to trust. The requirement statements are here because they are the text the FR
identifiers of Chapter 06 were derived from.

\subsection{The provenance of the bibliography}

The bibliography is annexed as a record, not as a list, because how it was assembled determines
what it can be used to say. On 2026-11-03 each of the 46 entries was chased through the same six
routes, in the same order, and only through legal ones: the DOI or URL declared in the entry,
Crossref for metadata, OpenAlex for an open-access location, Unpaywall for a declared legal copy,
a legal repository or a Wayback capture of the publisher's own PDF, and finally the publisher's
public record page. No payment, no shadow library, no institutional account. A file counted as
read only if it showed the body: a PDF had to yield method, results, conclusion and references
through \code{pdftotext}, and an HTML copy had to be the whole article rather than its summary.
The verdicts are 39 read in full, 2 partially, 4 abstract-only, and 1 inaccessible.

Two facts matter more than the count. First, the files are gone — they lived in a temporary
directory — so the ledger records file names, sizes and page counts per verdict; the proof of
reading is a measurement, not a path that still resolves. Second, crossing the verdict with the
character of the source gives a less flattering split: of the 20 academic works, 16 were read in
full, and the four that were not sit in the theoretical framework and in methodology, where an
omission costs most. Of the 18 vendor pages and READMEs, 17 were read in full, and that buys
nothing: a vendor blog read end to end is still a vendor blog, and it can support a claim about
what a vendor says, never a measurement of the system.

What was encountered and not circumvented belongs in the record too. Cloudflare stood in front of
the Springer, Wiley, SAGE, Elsevier, ISO and Taylor \& Francis platforms, \code{perfdrive} filtered
the Banco de la República site, and an Anubis proof-of-work challenge guarded EconStor; none of
the three was worked around. Where a legal copy existed, the alternative was taken instead of the
detour: Kovacs through the REAL repository, Menberu through the publisher's CC BY version, Miao
and Amagir through Wayback captures of the publisher's PDFs. Where none existed the entry stays
short, and Table~\ref{tab:annex-unread} says so one by one. No general web search was available,
so location relied on OpenAlex, Unpaywall and Semantic Scholar; one of the seven may have a legal
copy that only a search engine would have surfaced, which is a limit of the search, not a property
of the works.

From the ledger follows the rule the chapters were written under, which is why the ledger is
annexed: \emph{a work may be cited for its existence, or for a fact visible on the page actually
read; not for an argument if it was not read in full}. The rule costs something, and the cost is
visible. The ISO standard on usability is cited for the fact that a standard defines usability and
for the scope its public abstract states, never for a clause. The Banco de la República report is
cited for its authorship and for the 16.4~\% figure in its abstract, never for its sampling design.
Brooke is cited for the fact that the SUS was proposed in 1996 and for nothing else; no chapter
relies on the instrument's wording, its scoring rule, or any of its pages. The clearest instance
of the rule biting is a pagination conflict left open: the publisher's record gives pages 207-212,
the usability literature 189-194, and the entry in \code{referencias.bib} omits the range rather
than choose one without having seen the printed volume. Picking the more frequently quoted number
would have been a false datum dressed as formatting.

The ledger is not left as an agreement between people. \code{verify-citations.mjs} turns it into a
gate: a cited key absent from the \code{.bib} is an error, because biblatex would print a question
mark; a cited key whose verdict is not \enquote{read in full} raises a warning with the verdict in
view; a key with no verdict raises one too, because nobody has measured that source. Seven entries
are listed in Table~\ref{tab:annex-unread} with the reason each stopped, because hiding them would
be the one thing a reviewer could not forgive: a report that claims 46 sources and shows 46
abstracts is a different report.

\subsection{Re-measuring what the report claims}

Every number Chapter 11 quotes comes from a command that exists in the repository. A reviewer who
cannot run them has to take them on trust, which is not a position a thesis should put a reader
in. The listing below gives the entry point for each measurement, not a manual: the
parameters of the load profile, the account pool it needs and the thresholds it asserts are
documented in \code{deploy/loadtest/README.md}, and repeating them here would produce a second
copy to drift.

\begin{verbatim}
# The report (Docker, texlive/texlive:latest, latexmk + biber)
cd docs/informe && ./build.sh   # add force after editing the .bib

# The stack the measurements ran against
dev/build && dev/up && dev/migrate && dev/seed

# Frontend suites
cd frontend && npm test              # Karma, headless Chrome, 311 tests
cd frontend && npx playwright test   # 59 specs, five widths

# Load profile (k6 has no host binary; it runs as a container)
docker run --rm --network host -i grafana/k6 run - \
  < deploy/loadtest/k6-scenarios.js

# Style barriers and the citation ledger
node docs/informe/scripts/prose-barrier.mjs
node frontend/scripts/design-debt.mjs --check
node docs/informe/scripts/verify-citations.mjs

# Static barriers: eslint, oxlint, security, formula help, events
cd frontend && npm run lint
\end{verbatim}

Three notes on those commands, learned by running them. The document build runs the container as
root on purpose: under this machine's idmapped bind mounts, passing the host user id with
\code{-u} fails with \enquote{Cannot write file main.aux}, so a reviewer who adds that flag is not
reproducing this build. The end-to-end suite needs the whole stack raised by \code{dev/up}, not a
frontend-only server, because a spec that cannot reach a real endpoint fails in a way that looks
like a product defect; it is also the accessibility gate, since its locators select by role and
accessible name. And a clean build is not evidence that a page renders: a logo mask passed
\code{npm run build} and broke the development server, which is why the visual judgment in
Chapter 09 is treated as a test rather than a decoration.

\subsection{What this annex deliberately does not contain}

\textbf{No participant data.} The instrument set is a closed template: every table is empty, and
the emptiness is a decision. The instruments declare five debts that block the session phase — the
session script is unapproved, there are no seeded test accounts for the task that needs them, the
payment credentials the first task requires do not exist, the contact address on the consent form
is blank, and the mobile-width question is unanswered. Filling any of those with plausible numbers
would be fabrication of the kind that is hardest to detect, because it looks like work that was
done. What can be said without data is said in Chapter 13: which conditions the protocol would
have measured, and why none of them is learning. \emph{Preferring the new interface is not
evidence that more is learned} — the instruments measure efficacy, efficiency and perceived
satisfaction, and learning would need a validated pre- and post-test, a comparison group and, to
speak of effect, random assignment. That design is not in this work and is not simulated here.

\todo{The validation sessions have not been run: the contact address on the consent form is blank
and the session script is not yet approved, so Chapter 13 reports an empty protocol rather than
results.}

\textbf{No production traffic.} The load numbers come from a development host, and the load scripts
themselves warn that the same script pointed at the development topology measures the laptop. The
200-VU run is a characterization of behavior under a stated profile, not a capacity claim about a
deployed system; the autoscaler that declares a floor of two replicas in \code{deploy/k8s/base/}
and a ceiling of twenty in the production overlay is a different piece of evidence from a latency
percentile, and the report does not present one as the other.

\textbf{No personal data of anyone.} The consent form is a template with no name on it, the survey
asks for neither name nor email, and the capture sheets have no identifying column, by design.
There is consequently nothing here to anonymize, and the anonymity the instruments promise is a
property of the templates rather than of a later redaction step.

\textbf{No screenshots beyond \code{docs/informe/figures/}.} Six PNG files carry every screen this
document shows. The five UI kits under \code{design/ui_kits/} are not reproduced, because they are
HTML driven by a browser, and a screenshot of a page that can be opened is a lossy copy of it. The
same logic governs the two long artifacts: the instrument set and the reference list are pointed
at, not reprinted. The test of this annex is whether a reader can get from a sentence to the
artifact that supports it in one step. Where that step is missing, the sentence — not the annex —
is what needs fixing.
